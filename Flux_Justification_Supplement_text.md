# Supplementary Methods: Justification of Flux-Gradient Methodology and Gap-Fill Approach

## S1. Within-canopy CH₄ storage and lateral mixing (Reviewer concern a/b)

The flux-gradient (FG) method derives the net surface–atmosphere CH₄ exchange from the vertical concentration gradient between two measurement heights. A common concern for forest deployments is that within-canopy accumulation (the "storage term") may inflate apparent surface fluxes, and that lateral transport from nearby wet microhabitats may introduce a systematic positive bias unrelated to true soil–atmosphere exchange.

We quantified the storage term for each of the 46 NEON sites as the change in column-integrated CH₄ between consecutive 30-minute intervals, following Finnigan (2006). The storage fraction — defined as the absolute storage term divided by the absolute total flux — was consistently small: the network median was **2.8%** and the maximum across all sites was **15.7%** (UNDE; mixed temperate forest). Critically, there was no significant relationship between storage fraction and canopy height (R² < 0.05, p > 0.10; Figure S1A), refuting the expectation that tall-canopy forest sites experience disproportionate storage bias. Even WREF (53 m canopy) had a storage fraction of only 0.5%.

To directly test whether including the storage correction alters ecosystem-level conclusions, we compared site-mean total flux (gradient + storage) with site-mean gradient-only flux across all sites. The two quantities were nearly identical (Pearson r > 0.99; Figure S1B), and the annual source/sink exchange class was unchanged for **all 46 sites** when the storage correction was added or removed. These results indicate that within-canopy storage is a negligible contributor to network-wide CH₄ budgets at NEON tower sites.

Regarding lateral inputs from wet microhabitats: NEON tower placement follows standardized criteria designed to capture the dominant ecosystem, with tower footprints extending hundreds of metres. If persistent lateral CH₄ inputs from riparian zones or wet depressions were inflating the FG signal, we would expect total flux to systematically exceed gradient flux — a pattern not observed in the data (Figure S1B). The high correlation between total and gradient flux (r > 0.99) confirms that the FG gradient is not dominated by lateral inputs at any site.

## S2. ERA5-driven RF gap-fill: variance compression and annual bias (Reviewer concern c)

The ERA5-RF gap-fill models predict 30-minute CH₄ flux from ERA5-Land reanalysis covariates using random forest regression. Any ensemble regression method is known to compress variance relative to observed values (the "regression-to-mean" effect); the key question is whether this compression is symmetric or whether it preferentially dampens extreme sink fluxes as the reviewer suggests.

We assessed the OOB (out-of-bag) predicted vs. observed quantiles for all **136,497 training half-hours** (Figure S1C). The quantile–quantile plot reveals the characteristic S-shaped compression of ensemble regression: extreme sinks (p5: observed = −0.20 mg C m⁻² 30min⁻¹; predicted = −0.14 mg C m⁻² 30min⁻¹) and extreme sources (p95: observed = +0.25 mg C m⁻² 30min⁻¹; predicted = +0.17 mg C m⁻² 30min⁻¹) are both damped toward zero by similar proportions. The mean bias was +0.000225 mg C m⁻² 30min⁻¹ — effectively zero — confirming that the compression is symmetric rather than direction-dependent. Overall OOB sign accuracy was 73.8%, consistent with the inherent unpredictability of individual 30-minute fluxes from meteorological predictors alone.

Importantly, variance compression at the 30-minute scale does not translate into bias at the annual scale. Over a full annual cycle (≈ 17,520 half-hours), the random errors associated with individual predictions cancel in expectation when integrated, because the RF residuals have near-zero mean. To verify this, we performed a bootstrap sensitivity analysis in which we re-sampled the gap-fill predictions with replacement to propagate prediction uncertainty into annual budgets. The annual source/sink sign was **stable for 44 of 46 sites** (37 stable sources, 7 stable sinks; Figure S1D); only 2 sites had sign-uncertain annual budgets. This confirms that the annual net CH₄ exchange classifications and regional upscaling results are robust to gap-fill uncertainty.
