# Methods

## NEON 30-minute total CH4 flux processing and behavior classification

We analyzed NEON tower-gradient methane (CH4) fluxes using the total-flux product generated in `flow.30min.analysis.R`. The workflow read the repository CH4 flux table, retained NEON observations with valid timestamps and site identifiers, and constructed a total flux for each half-hour observation as:

`flux_total = FG_ENSEMBLE_RSHP + storage_flux_filled`.

The ensemble gradient term represents the turbulent gradient-flux estimate, and the storage term accounts for temporal changes in CH4 stored below the measurement height. The total flux was converted to mg C m-2 30 min-1 using the conversion factor used throughout the workflow, `2 * 0.0000288872 * 1000`. The resulting analysis variable was `CH4_mgC_30min`. Positive values were interpreted as net CH4 source states to the atmosphere, and negative values were interpreted as net CH4 sink states. For cross-scale driver analyses only, 30-minute fluxes were also expressed as umol C m-2 s-1 by converting mg C per 30 min to umol C per second with a 30 min integration time.

Sampling of half-hour observations was not uniform among sites, months, or times of day, so site behavior was not classified from raw row counts. Instead, observations were summarized in a balanced site-month-hour framework. For each site and month, total CH4 fluxes were first averaged within each half-hour-of-day bin (`hour_num`, 48 possible bins). Monthly source/sink state was then calculated from the mean of those equally weighted half-hour bins, so a heavily sampled part of the day could not dominate the monthly classification. Site behavior classes were assigned from the fraction of months with positive balanced monthly mean total flux. Sites with at least 75% positive months were classified as consistent sources, sites with no more than 25% positive months were classified as consistent sinks, and all other sites were classified as fluctuating. This design preserves subdaily information while reducing bias from uneven sampling and avoiding the treatment of serially correlated 30-minute observations as independent site-level evidence.

We also fit 30-minute generalized additive models (GAMs) to describe short-term flux dynamics. The continuous model used `CH4_mgC_30min` as the response, and the source-state model used a binomial response, `source_30min`, equal to one when total flux was positive. Both models included smooth or cyclic terms for hour of day, air temperature (`Tair_C`), soil volumetric water content (`VSWCMean`), and log-transformed photosynthetically active radiation (`log_PAR`), a tensor interaction between temperature and soil moisture, fixed effects for season and ecosystem type, and a site random effect (`s(SITE_ID, bs = "re")`). The diel model also used sine and cosine hour terms to capture periodic daily structure. These 30-minute GAMs were used to evaluate environmental and diel structure in the half-hourly data and to support later gapfilling; primary site-level behavior interpretations were based on the balanced monthly summaries rather than on individual half-hour rows.

## Site-level driver comparison for consistent source and fluctuating behavior

We used `NEON.StrongSink.DriverComparison.R` to compare site attributes between observed total-flux behavior classes. The original intent of this script was to contrast strong sinks with sources, but the updated total-flux classification did not identify any consistent sink sites at the observed 30-minute behavior-class scale. Therefore, the reported comparison contrasts consistent source sites with fluctuating sites.

The driver matrix combined static or slowly varying site attributes with site-level summaries of moisture variability. Candidate drivers included climate variables such as mean annual temperature and precipitation, soil chemistry and stoichiometry variables such as acidity, carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, and C:N ratio, soil physical variables such as dry mass and texture, and vegetation/canopy variables where available. Moisture summaries were computed from the same equal-weighted site-month-hour framework used for behavior classification before calculating site-level mean moisture, seasonal variability, and ranges. This kept moisture contrasts from being driven by sites or seasons with denser half-hour sampling.

For each driver, the script reported class medians, difference direction, Cliff's delta as a nonparametric effect-size measure, nominal Wilcoxon/Kruskal-style p-values, and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-values. These tests were treated as exploratory screening rather than confirmatory inference because the number of sites was modest, driver variables were correlated, and behavior class is itself an estimated site-level summary.

## MDS-style daily gap filling and annual budgets

We used `flow.30min.analysis.R` to estimate native-scale daily and annual CH4 budgets from observed half-hourly total fluxes using an MDS-style hierarchical lookup gap-filling procedure. This analysis was designed to convert unevenly sampled half-hour observations into complete daily totals and annual site budgets without using ERA5 meteorological forcing. For each observed site-date, the workflow constructed a complete 48-slot half-hour grid. Observed half-hours were retained directly, and missing half-hour slots were filled from progressively broader empirical lookup means.

The lookup hierarchy prioritized temporal and site specificity. Missing values were filled first from the same site, month, and half-hour of day; then from site-season-hour, site-bi-season-hour, and site-annual-hour means. Remaining gaps were filled from all-site month-hour, season-hour, bi-season-hour, annual-hour, site annual mean, and finally all-site mean values if needed. Total, gradient, and storage CH4 components were filled in parallel using the same selected lookup level. Daily total fluxes were then calculated by summing the 48 half-hour values and expressing the result as mg C m-2 d-1.

Annual MDS-style budgets were calculated by summing daily total fluxes within each site-year and converting from mg C m-2 yr-1 to g C m-2 yr-1. Site-level annual budgets were summarized as the mean across available site-years. Daily behavior was classified from the fraction of filled daily fluxes that were positive, and annual behavior was classified from the fraction of site-years with positive annual budgets. The same thresholds were used throughout: sites were classified as consistent sources if at least 75% of days or years were positive, consistent sinks if no more than 25% were positive, and fluctuating otherwise.

The primary annual-budget results in this draft are based on these MDS-style daily-to-annual products from `flow.30min.analysis.R`. ERA5-based annual budgets were treated separately as an independent sensitivity comparison, not as the main annual-budget estimate.

## ERA5-driven half-hourly gap-filling

We used `NEON.ERA5.HalfHourlyGapfill.R` to produce a continuous half-hourly CH4 flux record at each NEON site by combining observed total-flux half-hours with random forest (RF) model predictions driven by ERA5 reanalysis meteorology. The workflow obtained hourly ERA5 point covariates for each NEON tower coordinate through the Open-Meteo Archive API and cached the results in `OUTPUT/NEON_ERA5_hourly_site_covariates.csv.gz`. Extracted variables included 2 m air temperature, 0–7 cm and 7–28 cm soil volumetric water content, soil temperature, accumulated precipitation, incoming shortwave radiation, and vapor pressure deficit. Hourly values were linearly interpolated to the half-hour grid and joined to observed half-hourly flux records.

Before fitting the RF, we constructed a climatological lookup table (LUT) predictor from the observed total fluxes. For each half-hour, a site mean flux was first sought from the same site, calendar month, and half-hour-of-day bin. When fewer than three observations were available at that resolution, the search relaxed successively to site-season-hour, site-hour, and finally global-month-hour means, with the first non-missing level selected. The resulting variable, `climatological_CH4_mgC_30min`, captures the typical flux magnitude at that site and time of day while smoothly handling sparse site-months.

Two RF models were fit using the `ranger` package in R. The site model included `SITE_ID` as a predictor and was used to evaluate within-site predictive performance; the population model omitted `SITE_ID` and was used for prediction across ERA5 grid cells where no tower observations exist. Both models used the same seventeen predictors: ERA5 air temperature, shallow and deep soil volumetric water content, soil temperature, precipitation, shortwave radiation, vapor pressure deficit, seven-day rolling mean air temperature, seven-day rolling mean precipitation, an air temperature by soil moisture interaction term, the climatological LUT flux, sine and cosine cyclic terms for hour of day and day of year, and ecosystem type (EcoType). The `mtry` hyperparameter was tuned by minimizing OOB mean squared error over a candidate grid, and the final population model used 500 trees. Gap-filled half-hourly fluxes retained observed values where available and substituted population RF predictions for missing half-hours. The complete gap-filled record was written to `OUTPUT/NEON_ERA5_gapfilled_30min.csv.gz`.

Annual ERA5 budgets were calculated by summing gap-filled half-hour fluxes within each site-year and converting from mg C m⁻² 30 min⁻¹ to g C m⁻² yr⁻¹. Site-mean annual budgets were compared with balanced site-month-hour LUT annual budgets from `NEON.30min.Gapfill.R` as an independent check on sign and magnitude. Site-level ERA5 annual behavior was classified from the fraction of site-years with positive annual budgets using the same 75% and 25% thresholds applied throughout.

## Monthly RF spatial upscaling

We used `ERA-SpatialUpscaling-Monthly-RF.R` to upscale ERA5-driven monthly CH4 exchange rates to global non-wetland upland land area using a two-stage RF framework. The gap-filled 30-minute file (`OUTPUT/NEON_ERA5_gapfilled_30min.csv.gz`) was read in full and monthly flux totals were aggregated on the fly by summing `gapfilled_CH4_mgC_30min` within each SITE_ID, year, and calendar month, producing monthly training budgets in mg C m⁻² month⁻¹.

The first stage classified each site-month as a weak source or weak sink using a balanced RF. Of 2,052 available site-month observations, 425 sink and 425 source site-months were sampled with equal probability, producing 850 balanced training observations. Six predictors were used: mean annual precipitation (MAP), mean annual temperature (MAT), aridity index, monthly mean ERA5 air temperature, monthly mean ERA5 soil volumetric water content, and ecosystem type. The RF was fit with `ranger` using 500 trees. Out-of-bag class probabilities were then calibrated with isotonic regression to remove OOB probability inflation before being applied to held-out grid cells. Calibrated P(source) values propagated through all downstream flux expression steps.

The second stage estimated flux magnitude separately for sink-month and source-month observations using two RF regression models. Monthly absolute flux values were log-transformed before fitting and back-transformed for prediction to maintain positive magnitudes. The sink magnitude model was trained on the 425 balanced sink observations and the source magnitude model on the 1,627 source observations available before balancing. The same six predictors were used for magnitude as for class prediction.

Monthly flux predictions were generated for each 0.5° × 0.5° ERA5 land-surface grid cell over global non-wetland upland area from 2000 to 2025. Three flux-expression approaches were computed for each cell-month. The Continuous approach combined sink and source magnitudes weighted by calibrated probability: P(source) × source_rate − (1 − P(source)) × |sink_rate|. The Dichotomous approach assigned each cell-month to whichever class had the higher calibrated probability and applied the corresponding magnitude. The All-Sink approach applied the sink magnitude to every cell-month regardless of class probability, representing the maximum-sink constrained scenario. Monthly cell fluxes were scaled to Tg CH₄ yr⁻¹ using grid-cell area and a 12/16 C-to-CH₄ mass conversion, then summed across cells and months for annual global totals. Spatial maps of predicted flux were written via `ERA-SpatialUpscaling-Figures-RF.R` from `cell_preds` objects saved in `Upscaling_Monthly_RF/OUTPUT/`.

## Cross-scale driver analysis

We used `NEON.DriverScale.Analysis.R` to evaluate drivers of source probability and flux magnitude at three temporal scales: 30-minute, daily, and annual. The central design choice was to separate within-site temporal drivers from among-site trait and climate drivers. Without this separation, a model could attribute persistent differences among sites to short-term temporal processes, or attribute temporal dynamics to static site differences.

The script generated three model-ready tables. The 30-minute table, `OUTPUT/NEON_driver_scale_30min_driver_data.csv`, used observed total CH4 fluxes from `OUTPUT/30min_ch4_model_data.csv`. Fluxes were represented in their native 30-minute driver-analysis units as umol C m-2 s-1, and source state was defined as flux greater than zero. The primary daily table, `OUTPUT/NEON_driver_scale_daily_driver_data.csv`, used lookup-filled daily total fluxes from `OUTPUT/NEON_scale_daily_flux_all_sites.csv`. Daily flux was calculated on a complete 48 half-hour grid for each observed site-date: observed half-hours were retained, missing half-hours were filled from site month-hour, season-hour, bi-season-hour, and annual-hour means, and the completed values were summed to mg C m-2 d-1. The annual table, `OUTPUT/NEON_driver_scale_annual_era5_driver_data.csv`, used ERA5-gapfilled site-year annual budgets from `OUTPUT/NEON_ERA5_gapfilled_annual_budget_by_year.csv` and annual ERA5 covariates summarized from the half-hour ERA5 driver table.

For each scale, two responses were modeled. Source probability was modeled with a binomial response indicating whether the flux or budget was positive. Flux magnitude was modeled as `log10(abs(flux) + 1e-9)` so that source and sink magnitudes could be analyzed together while avoiding undefined log values near zero. The 30-minute and daily models centered temporal drivers within site. For example, the 30-minute table included `Tair_C_within_site`, `VSWCMean_within_site`, and `log_PAR_within_site`, calculated as each observation minus that site's mean for the corresponding variable. The paired site means were retained for interpretation but the subdaily and daily model formulas emphasized the within-site temporal components. This made the smooth temperature, moisture, and light effects interpretable as short-term deviations from a site's usual conditions.

The 30-minute source-probability and magnitude models included smooth effects of within-site air temperature, within-site soil moisture, within-site log(PAR), cyclic hour of day, cyclic day of year, fixed effects for season and ecosystem type, and a site random effect. The daily models used smooth effects of within-site daily mean air temperature, soil moisture, and log(PAR), cyclic day of year, season, ecosystem type, and a site random effect. The annual models used linear terms for ERA5 annual temperature and moisture anomalies within site, ERA5 site-mean temperature and moisture, mean annual precipitation, soil acidity, soil carbon, soil sulfur, and ecosystem type. Annual models were intentionally kept simpler because the annual data set contained far fewer observations and repeated site-years; dependence among repeated site-years was handled with site-level bootstrap weights rather than by fitting a saturated site random-effect structure.

Models were fit with `mgcv`, using `bam` for larger data sets and `gam` for smaller data sets, with REML/fREML smoothing selection and shrinkage selection enabled. Effect uncertainty and variable importance were estimated with a blocked/site-level bootstrap: bootstrap replicates resampled sites with replacement and then refit the model using the induced site weights, so uncertainty reflected among-site leverage rather than treating autocorrelated rows as independent. Variable importance was summarized from full-model term statistics for each bootstrap replicate rather than from drop-one refits. Partial-effect curves were generated by varying one focal driver across its observed range while holding other covariates at typical values and excluding site random effects where present. The current draft outputs used a small bootstrap count for inspection; final inference should use a larger bootstrap count.

## Comparison with FLUXNET-CH4, soil-chamber, and process-model reference rates

We used `NEON.FLUXNET.CH4FluxComparison.R` to compare ERA5-gapfilled NEON annual gradient CH4 budgets with published ecosystem-scale and soil-based reference rates. NEON annual budgets from `OUTPUT/NEON_ERA5_gapfilled_annual_budget_by_year.csv` were converted to daily-equivalent rates as annual g C m-2 yr-1 * 1000 / 365 = mg C m-2 d-1. Site-level NEON values were summarized as medians across available ERA5-gapfilled site-years and grouped by ERA5 annual behavior class and ecosystem type. This benchmark comparison is therefore an ERA5-based magnitude comparison, separate from the primary MDS-style annual-budget results.

Published FLUXNET-CH4 ecosystem-class annual means and standard deviations from Delwiche et al. (2021) were converted from g C m-2 yr-1 to mg C m-2 d-1 by multiplying by 1000 and dividing by 365. Upland soil CH4 uptake estimates from the MeMo v1.0 process model (Murguia-Flores et al. 2018) were converted from mg CH4 m-2 yr-1 to mg C m-2 d-1 by multiplying by 12/16, dividing by 365, and assigning a negative sign to match the NEON convention that uptake is negative.

We also compiled soil-chamber literature benchmarks as an additional comparison and wrote them to `OUTPUT/soil_chamber_CH4_flux_reference_values.csv`. These included wetland chamber emissions from Whalen (2005), forest soil uptake from Adamsen and King (1993) and Groffman and Pouyat (2009), desert soil uptake from Striegl et al. (1992), grassland/aerobic soil uptake from Mosier et al. (1991), and cropland chamber fluxes from a Danish farmland chamber study. All chamber values were converted to mg C m-2 d-1, with positive values indicating net emission and negative values indicating uptake. The main comparison figure overlays ERA5-gapfilled NEON site and ecosystem-group summaries, FLUXNET ecosystem classes, MeMo process-model uptake rates, and the soil-chamber benchmarks so that NEON tower-gradient annual rates can be evaluated against both ecosystem-scale and soil-scale reference magnitudes.

# Results

## NEON site behavior and MDS-based annual budgets

The native-scale NEON analysis included 39 sites spanning forests, grasslands, shrublands, croplands, and one wetland site. Observed total-flux behavior at the balanced monthly 30-minute scale separated sites into two classes: 21 consistent sources and 18 fluctuating sites. No site was classified as a consistent sink from the observed monthly half-hour-balanced total-flux summaries. The gradient-only classification was similar but not identical, with 20 consistent source sites and 19 fluctuating sites, indicating that storage flux altered the observed class assignment for a small number of sites but did not create any observed consistent sink class.

Daily MDS-style gap filling shifted some sites into clearer daily source or sink behavior by completing the 48 half-hour grid for each observed site-date. Across all site-date half-hour slots used in the daily product, 15.0% were observed directly, 74.5% were filled from site-month-hour lookup means, 7.57% from site-season-hour means, 1.73% from site-bi-season-hour means, 0.80% from site-annual-hour means, and 0.35% from all-site month-hour means. No broader all-site season, bi-season, annual-hour, site annual mean, or all-site mean fallbacks were needed. Daily behavior classified 28 sites as consistent sources, 10 as fluctuating, and one as a consistent sink.

Annual MDS-style budgets were calculated by summing the gap-filled daily totals within site-years and averaging across available years for each site. The annual classification identified 32 consistent source sites, three fluctuating sites, and four consistent sink sites. Annual consistent sources included 16 forests, eight grasslands, five shrublands, two croplands, and one wetland. Annual consistent sinks included three forests and one grassland, and all three annual fluctuating sites were forests.

MDS-based annual budgets were small in absolute magnitude but mostly positive. Across sites, mean annual budgets ranged from -0.113 to 1.09 g C m-2 yr-1, with a median of 0.0988 g C m-2 yr-1 and mean of 0.136 g C m-2 yr-1. The largest annual source budgets were estimated for LAJA (1.09 g C m-2 yr-1), GRSM (0.454 g C m-2 yr-1), OAES (0.412 g C m-2 yr-1), NOGP (0.384 g C m-2 yr-1), LENO (0.263 g C m-2 yr-1), SJER (0.243 g C m-2 yr-1), TALL (0.229 g C m-2 yr-1), and MOAB (0.216 g C m-2 yr-1). The strongest annual sinks were UKFS (-0.113 g C m-2 yr-1), WREF (-0.0848 g C m-2 yr-1), BONA (-0.0521 g C m-2 yr-1), and DCFS (-0.0412 g C m-2 yr-1).

These results show that the observed 30-minute summaries captured frequent weak source behavior, while the MDS-style daily-to-annual aggregation converted many sites into net annual sources. The annual budgets remained much smaller than typical wetland ecosystem CH4 emissions, but the annual class structure indicates that weak positive half-hourly and daily fluxes accumulated into positive annual budgets at most NEON sites.

## Site attributes associated with observed behavior

Site-level driver comparisons suggested that consistent source sites tended to differ from fluctuating sites in soil physical and chemical properties and climate. Consistent source sites had lower dry mass than fluctuating sites (median 2.25 versus 3.41; Cliff's delta = -0.411; nominal p = 0.029), lower pH/acidity index values (median 8.08 versus 17.4; Cliff's delta = -0.389; nominal p = 0.039), and higher mean annual temperature (median 12.9 versus 8.6 degrees C; Cliff's delta = 0.366; nominal p = 0.053). C:N ratio was also lower in consistent source sites than fluctuating sites (median 8.98 versus 14.1; nominal p = 0.058). After adjustment for multiple comparisons, these contrasts were exploratory rather than confirmatory, but they suggest that warmer sites with lower dry mass and lower soil acidity/chemistry indices were more likely to behave as consistent CH4 sources.

## ERA5 RF gap-filling performance

The half-hourly RF gap-filling model was trained on 136,497 observed half-hour flux records from 46 NEON sites. The population model (omitting SITE_ID) achieved an out-of-bag R² of 0.191 and a corresponding RMSE of approximately 0.210 mg C m⁻² 30 min⁻¹. Thirty-minute sign accuracy—the fraction of half-hours for which the model correctly predicted the direction of flux—was 73.9%. At the annual scale, ERA5 budget signs agreed with the balanced site-month-hour LUT annual budgets from `NEON.30min.Gapfill.R` for 34 of 39 sites. Sites where ERA5 and LUT annual budgets disagreed in sign were BARR, BONA, GUAN, HARV, and TOOL; STEI showed a near-zero discrepancy that was sensitive to rounding. These disagreements were concentrated at sites with annual budgets close to zero, where the gap-filling approach strongly influences whether cumulative exchange is positive or negative. Across all sites, annual behavior classes assigned from ERA5 gap-filled budgets closely matched those from LUT budgets for clear sources and sinks, with discrepancies primarily affecting sites near the source-sink boundary.

## Monthly RF spatial upscaling

The stage-1 balanced RF distinguished source from sink site-months with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.914, a Brier skill score (BSS) of 0.542, and an OOB accuracy of 84.1% at the 0.5 probability threshold (sensitivity 81.2%, specificity 87.1%). These metrics indicate that long-term climate and ecosystem context were strong determinants of monthly source-sink state. Predictor importance, measured by OOB permutation, ranked MAP first (38.0% relative importance), followed by MAT (36.2%), aridity index (32.2%), monthly ERA5 air temperature (26.0%), monthly ERA5 soil volumetric water content (17.9%), and ecosystem type (9.7%). The dominance of long-term climate indices over within-month ERA5 conditions indicates that source-sink classification at the monthly scale was controlled primarily by the mean climate envelope of the site rather than by short-term meteorological anomalies.

Stage-2 magnitude models demonstrated strong predictive performance for both flux directions. The sink-month RF achieved a correlation of 0.859 between predicted and observed monthly flux magnitudes (RMSE = 0.0229 g C m⁻² month⁻¹; n = 425). The source-month RF achieved a correlation of 0.896 (RMSE = 0.0297 g C m⁻² month⁻¹; n = 1,627). State-magnitude agreement between the RF class predictions and observed state was 92.4% for forests, 92.6% for grasslands, and 88.6% for shrublands, and the magnitude sign was correct in 100% of cases for all three ecosystem types evaluated. These fits indicate that monthly flux magnitudes were largely predictable from the same six predictors used for class discrimination, with ecosystem type and mean climate conditions accounting for most of the systematic variation in magnitude.

For the 2000–2025 upscaling period, mean global non-wetland upland CH4 exchange was +41.4 Tg CH₄ yr⁻¹ under the Continuous approach (range 40.6–42.0), +50.0 Tg CH₄ yr⁻¹ under the Dichotomous approach (range 48.8–50.8), and −14.2 Tg CH₄ yr⁻¹ under the All-Sink approach (range −13.5 to −15.0). All three estimates were remarkably stable across the 26-year ERA5 period, indicating that interannual ERA5 variability did not substantially shift the global partitioning of source and sink area or magnitude. The Continuous and Dichotomous approaches produced net positive estimates, opposite in sign to the Global Methane Budget (GMB) terrestrial soil uptake estimate of −35 Tg CH₄ yr⁻¹ (Saunois et al. 2020). The All-Sink approach produced a net sink estimate of −14.2 Tg CH₄ yr⁻¹—consistent in sign with the GMB reference but smaller in magnitude. The difference between the All-Sink estimate and the GMB reference partly reflects the fact that the GMB soil sink is estimated predominantly from soil-chamber observations, which by design sample soil oxidation and are likely biased toward uptake relative to ecosystem-scale net exchange that integrates all CH₄ pathways. NEON tower-gradient measurements capture net ecosystem exchange at the landscape scale, including episodic source behavior that chamber campaigns are unlikely to capture, and thus may resolve a different quantity than the chamber-based GMB estimate.

Figure 4 shows global maps of predicted 2025 monthly CH4 flux summed to an annual rate under each of the three flux-expression approaches. Panel A (Continuous) and Panel B (Dichotomous) show broadly positive exchange across tropical and subtropical land areas, with the strongest source signal in warm, moderately moist regions where the calibrated P(source) was high. Panel C (All-Sink) shows a globally negative spatial pattern differentiated by ecosystem type, ERA5 temperature, and soil moisture, with stronger uptake in temperate forests and lower magnitude uptake in drier regions. The contrast among panels illustrates how the flux-expression assumption—whether to allow the NEON-trained source probability to propagate across un-sampled global grid cells—controls both the sign and spatial distribution of estimated non-wetland upland CH4 exchange. The three panels represent a range of interpretations of the NEON tower-gradient training data, from a model that treats towers as representative of upland CH4 exchange (Continuous, Dichotomous) to a maximum-uptake constrained baseline (All-Sink).

## Cross-scale source probability and magnitude drivers

The cross-scale driver analysis confirmed that source probability and flux magnitude are scale-dependent. At the 30-minute scale, the source-probability model used 131,354 observations from 39 sites and explained 5.46% of deviance; the log absolute magnitude model explained 25.1% of deviance. At the daily scale, the source-probability model used 18,242 site-days from 39 sites and explained 6.77% of deviance; the magnitude model explained 21.6% of deviance. At the annual scale, the source-probability and magnitude models used 150 site-years from 39 sites and explained 48.0% and 22.8% of deviance, respectively.

Within-site temporal drivers dominated the 30-minute and daily models. For source probability, within-site PAR, soil moisture, air temperature, and day of year were supported at both 30-minute and daily scales, while hour of day was supported only at the 30-minute scale. For flux magnitude, within-site PAR, air temperature, soil moisture, and day of year were supported at both 30-minute and daily scales; hour of day again acted primarily at the 30-minute scale. Ecosystem type was supported across 30-minute, daily, and annual analyses for both source probability and flux magnitude, indicating a persistent among-site component that was not reducible to short-term temporal anomalies.

Annual-scale driver patterns differed from the subdaily and daily models. Annual flux magnitude was associated with ecosystem type, ERA5 site-mean soil moisture and air temperature, MAP, acidity, and ERA5 annual anomalies. Annual source probability was associated primarily with ecosystem type and among-site variables including acidity, MAP, ERA5 site-mean soil moisture, ERA5 site-mean air temperature, and carbon. However, because the annual analysis included only 150 site-years and used ERA5-gapfilled annual budgets, these annual-scale associations should be interpreted as predictors of net annual source outcome rather than direct short-term process controls.

## Comparison with published CH4 flux benchmarks

ERA5-gapfilled NEON annual daily-equivalent CH4 fluxes were small relative to published FLUXNET-CH4 ecosystem-class fluxes. The NEON ERA5 consistent source class had a median site-level daily-equivalent flux of 1.16 mg C m-2 d-1, with an interquartile range of 0.665 to 1.56 mg C m-2 d-1. The single ERA5 fluctuating site had a median of 0.027 mg C m-2 d-1, and the ERA5 consistent sink class had a median of -0.441 mg C m-2 d-1, with an interquartile range of -0.554 to -0.308 mg C m-2 d-1. By contrast, published FLUXNET-CH4 ecosystem-class means ranged from 7.95 mg C m-2 d-1 for salt marshes to 127 mg C m-2 d-1 for urban sites, with marshes, lakes, swamps, fens, rice systems, mangroves, and bogs all exceeding NEON ERA5 class medians by one to several orders of magnitude.

Soil-chamber benchmarks placed NEON fluxes closer to upland soil uptake and weak soil-source rates than to wetland ecosystem emissions. Wetland soil chamber emissions were represented by a benchmark of 75 mg C m-2 d-1, again far above NEON class medians. In contrast, forest, desert, grassland, and cropland chamber benchmarks ranged from approximately -1.5 to -0.113 mg C m-2 d-1, overlapping the low-magnitude negative to weak-positive range of NEON ERA5 site medians. MeMo process-model uptake estimates were similarly negative and generally ranged from about -1.24 to -0.216 mg C m-2 d-1 after conversion to daily C units. These comparisons suggest that the NEON tower-gradient CH4 signal in this analysis is much weaker than typical wetland ecosystem-scale emissions and more comparable in magnitude to upland soil uptake or weak soil-atmosphere exchange.

## Overall interpretation

Across workflows, NEON CH4 behavior was best described as frequent switching between weak source and sink states superimposed on small net annual budgets. The 30-minute analysis identified short-term source-state structure and diel/environmental controls, but its rows are highly autocorrelated and should not be treated as independent site-level evidence. The MDS-style daily product provided the primary empirical bridge from uneven half-hourly observations to complete daily and annual budgets. MDS-style and ERA5 annual analyses both indicated that many sites were net annual sources, but annual classifications collapsed short-term switching into net budget outcomes and differed for a small set of near-zero sites. Therefore, daily source probability is the most appropriate scale for inference about source potential, while MDS-style annual budgets and ERA5 annual budgets are best interpreted as separate estimates of net annual source outcome.
