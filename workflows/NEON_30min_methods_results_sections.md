# Methods and Results Text for the 30-Minute NEON CH4 Analyses

## Methods

### Thirty-minute total-flux analysis

We analyzed 30-minute methane (CH4) fluxes using the updated total-flux product, defined as the sum of the gradient flux and storage flux. Fluxes were converted to mg C m-2 30 min-1 using the workflow conversion factor `2 * 0.0000288872 * 1000`. Positive total fluxes were interpreted as source states and negative total fluxes as sink states.

The 30-minute analysis combined flux observations with monthly soil moisture, site metadata, soil biogeochemistry, climate, and canopy attributes. Candidate covariates included hour of day, season, air temperature, volumetric soil water content, photosynthetically active radiation, ecosystem type, and site identity. Negative PAR values were set to zero and PAR was log-transformed as `log1p(PAR)`. Hour of day was represented both as a smooth term and with sine/cosine cyclic terms.

To reduce bias from uneven sampling across times of day, site behavior was not classified from the raw mean of all 30-minute observations. Instead, each site-month was first summarized within observed half-hour-of-day bins, and monthly means were calculated by averaging those bins with equal weight. Each month was then classified as a source month when the balanced monthly mean total CH4 flux was greater than zero. Sites were classified as consistent sources when at least 75% of observed months were source months, consistent sinks when no more than 25% of observed months were source months, and fluctuating otherwise.

Two generalized additive models were fit to the 30-minute observations using `mgcv::bam`. The continuous-flux model used a Gaussian response for total CH4 flux. The source-state model used a binomial response indicating whether each 30-minute observation was positive. Both models included smooth effects of hour of day, air temperature, soil water content, and log-transformed PAR, a tensor interaction between air temperature and soil water content, fixed effects for season and ecosystem type, and a random smooth for site. These models were used to describe diel and short-term environmental structure in flux magnitude and source probability; site behavior classes were interpreted from the balanced monthly summaries to avoid treating autocorrelated half-hour observations as independent site-level replicates.

### Model-standardized 30-minute gap filling and annual budgets

We used the fitted 30-minute total-flux GAM to infer site behavior and annual CH4 budgets under a balanced diel, seasonal, and covariate design. The prediction grid contained every site, all 12 months, and all 48 half-hour-of-day bins. For each site-month-hour cell, covariates were filled from observed site-month-hour medians when available. Missing cells were filled hierarchically using broader summaries: site-month, site-season-hour, site-season, site, global month-hour, global month, and finally global medians.

The fitted total-flux model was then used to predict expected CH4 flux for every grid cell. Monthly budgets were calculated by summing predicted half-hour fluxes across the 48 daily intervals and weighting by the number of days in each month. Annual budgets were calculated by summing across months. We propagated model-estimation uncertainty by drawing 1,000 coefficient vectors from the fitted GAM covariance matrix and recalculating monthly and annual budgets for each draw. Reported intervals are 95% simulation intervals from these coefficient draws and represent uncertainty in the expected model-standardized budget, not all sources of ecological or measurement uncertainty.

Standardized behavior classes were defined from the model-standardized monthly budgets using the same 75% source-month threshold as the observed balanced monthly classification. Sites were classified as consistent sources when at least 9 of 12 model-standardized months had positive mean budgets, consistent sinks when no more than 3 months were positive, and fluctuating otherwise.

### Consistency, timing, location, and magnitude analysis

To connect site behavior to interpretable timing, location, and magnitude patterns, we summarized the model-standardized monthly and annual outputs. Monthly heatmaps were used to show when each site was expected to be a CH4 source or sink through the year, using both mean monthly budget and monthly source probability. Annual budget plots were used to quantify expected magnitude and uncertainty for each site.

We then explored which site attributes were associated with consistency and magnitude. Annual budget outputs were joined with site-level attributes from the driver comparison and, where available, the expanded multivariate site-attribute matrix. Numeric predictors were retained when they had observations for at least 20 sites, no more than 40% missing values, and nonzero variance. Missing predictor values were filled with the site-variable median for exploratory modeling.

Candidate predictors were screened by their maximum absolute univariate association with either annual CH4 budget or standardized source-month fraction, using Spearman rank correlations. A Kruskal-Wallis test was also used to screen differences between consistent and fluctuating groups. The 12 highest-ranking predictors were used in two small decision-tree models: a classification tree distinguishing consistent from fluctuating sites, and a regression tree predicting annual CH4 budget. Leave-one-site-out cross-validation was used as a simple out-of-sample check for both trees. These trees were treated as exploratory summaries of site-attribute space, not as final predictive or causal models.

### Monthly sink behavior analysis

Because site-level consistent sink classes were rare, we evaluated sink behavior at the site-month scale. Observed monthly sink states were defined from `OUTPUT/30min_monthly_site_ch4.csv`, where each site-month had already been summarized by equal-weighting observed half-hour-of-day bins. A site-month was classified as a sink month when the balanced monthly mean total CH4 flux was less than or equal to zero. Model-standardized sink months were defined from `OUTPUT/NON_30min_gapfill_monthly_budgets.csv`, where each site had a complete 12-month by 48-half-hour prediction grid; standardized sink months were months with predicted monthly budgets less than or equal to zero.

We summarized the frequency of sink months by calendar month, season, and site for both observed balanced monthly fluxes and model-standardized monthly budgets. Seasonal summaries were used to evaluate when sink behavior was most common, while site summaries were used to identify whether monthly sink states were concentrated in sites classified as fluctuating at the site level.

To identify the monthly conditions associated with sink states, we compared sink and source months using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. For observed balanced monthly data, candidate monthly conditions included mean air temperature, mean volumetric soil water content, within-site temperature anomaly, within-site soil-water anomaly, mean gradient flux component, mean storage flux component, storage absolute fraction, and the number of observed half-hour bins contributing to the monthly estimate. Within-site anomalies were calculated by subtracting each site's mean monthly value from the site-month value, allowing the analysis to ask whether sink months occurred during relatively warmer, cooler, wetter, or drier months within a site. For model-standardized monthly budgets, candidate conditions included standardized monthly air temperature, standardized VSWC, log(PAR + 1), and within-site anomalies for those covariates.

We also fit an exploratory monthly sink-state generalized additive model with a binomial response for observed monthly sink state. The model included season, smooth effects of within-site temperature anomaly, within-site VSWC anomaly, and number of observed half-hour bins, plus a random smooth for site identity. This model was used to check whether the monthly contrasts persisted after accounting for repeated observations within sites and uneven monthly sampling coverage. As with the site-attribute analyses, the monthly sink model was interpreted as exploratory and descriptive rather than causal.

### ERA5-driven half-hourly gap filling

As an independent check on the monthly-bin extrapolation, we created a second annual-budget estimate using continuous meteorological and soil-moisture covariates from ERA5. Hourly ERA5 point data were obtained for each NEON tower coordinate using the Open-Meteo Archive API. We extracted 2 m air temperature and 0-7 cm soil volumetric water content for the full set of calendar years represented in the flux data. Hourly ERA5 values were linearly interpolated to the half-hourly flux timestamp grid.

We fit a half-hourly total-flux GAM using observed 30-minute total CH4 flux as the response and ERA5 temperature and ERA5 soil volumetric water content as the main environmental predictors. The model included cyclic smooths for hour of day and day of year, smooth effects of ERA5 temperature and ERA5 soil moisture, a tensor interaction between ERA5 temperature and ERA5 soil moisture, season, ecosystem type, and a random smooth for site identity. Annual half-hourly budgets were then generated by retaining observed fluxes where available and filling missing half-hours with ERA5-driven model predictions. Annual budgets were calculated by summing half-hourly gapfilled fluxes within each site-year and converting to g C m-2 yr-1. Site-level ERA5 budgets were summarized as the mean annual budget across available years.

We compared the ERA5-driven half-hourly budgets to the model-standardized monthly-bin annual budgets from `NEON.30min.Gapfill.r`. The comparison used site-level mean annual budgets, Spearman rank correlation, mean bias, RMSE, source/sink sign agreement, and site-level budget differences. This comparison evaluates whether a half-hourly meteorological gapfill produces the same annual pattern as the simpler balanced monthly-bin extrapolation.

## Results

### Thirty-minute total-flux behavior and model structure

The filtered 30-minute total-flux data set contained 131,354 observations across 39 NEON sites. Based on the balanced monthly classification, 20 sites were classified as consistent sources and 19 sites were classified as fluctuating. No sites were classified as consistent sinks from the observed balanced monthly total-flux summaries. The switch from gradient-only flux to total flux did not change the observed monthly site classifications in this run: 20 sites remained consistent sources and 19 remained fluctuating.

The Gaussian 30-minute flux GAM explained a small but detectable fraction of half-hour flux variation (adjusted R2 = 0.023; deviance explained = 2.39%). Smooth terms for hour of day, air temperature, soil water content, log(PAR + 1), the air-temperature by soil-water interaction, and site identity were all significant. The binomial source-state model explained more variation in source probability than the continuous model explained in flux magnitude, but still only a modest fraction of the full 30-minute variability (deviance explained = 5.5%). Smooth terms for hour, air temperature, soil water content, log(PAR + 1), their temperature-moisture interaction, and site identity were all significant in the source-state model. These results indicate that diel timing, environmental covariates, and site identity contain meaningful information about short-term source probability, while much of the 30-minute flux magnitude remains unexplained at this scale.

Exploratory site-level contrasts suggested that observed consistent-source sites tended to differ from fluctuating sites most strongly in soil physical, soil chemical, and climate attributes. The clearest individual contrasts were lower dry mass, lower pH/acidity values, warmer mean annual temperature, lower C:N ratio, lower estimated organic carbon, and shallower biogeochemical top depth in consistent-source sites. The direction of these contrasts was coherent across several related soil variables: consistent-source sites generally had lower values for dry mass, C:N, estimated organic carbon, total C, sulfur, biogeochemical top depth, and biogeochemical bottom depth than fluctuating sites. These patterns suggest that observed fluctuating sites tended to occupy soils with greater mass or organic-matter-related values, whereas consistent-source sites tended to occur in warmer settings with lower values for several soil chemistry/depth metrics.

Moisture and canopy variables were weaker separators of the observed behavior categories than soil and climate variables. Mean soil-water-content variance, wetness seasonality, and mean VSWC all had smaller effect sizes than the leading soil contrasts. LAI was lower in consistent-source sites, while canopy height and canopy height model summaries tended to be higher in consistent-source sites, but these vegetation/canopy contrasts were modest relative to dry mass, acidity, mean annual temperature, and C:N ratio. These should be interpreted as descriptive site-attribute differences among classes rather than causal drivers, because the tests are univariate, site counts are limited, and many attributes covary.

### Model-standardized annual budgets and class changes

Balancing the design across all months and half-hour bins changed the inferred behavior for a subset of sites. Under the model-standardized grid, 33 sites were classified as consistent sources, 5 as fluctuating, and 1 as a consistent sink. Fourteen of 39 sites changed class relative to the observed balanced monthly classification. Thirteen sites changed from fluctuating to consistent source, and one site changed from fluctuating to consistent sink. The 20 sites classified as consistent sources in the observed balanced summaries remained consistent sources under the model-standardized analysis.

Annual model-standardized CH4 budgets ranged from -0.222 to 1.514 g C m-2 yr-1, with a median of 0.414 g C m-2 yr-1 and a mean of 0.442 g C m-2 yr-1. The only standardized consistent sink was DCFS, with an annual budget of -0.222 g C m-2 yr-1 (95% interval: -0.325 to -0.124). The largest positive annual budgets occurred at LENO (1.51 g C m-2 yr-1; 95% interval: 1.14 to 1.91), CLBJ (1.18; 0.755 to 1.61), GRSM (1.12; 0.966 to 1.27), NIWO (1.01; 0.474 to 1.54), and OAES (0.971; 0.889 to 1.05).

The class changes indicate that uneven measurement timing can obscure the expected behavior of some sites. Several sites that appeared fluctuating in the observed monthly summaries were predicted to be sources in at least 75% of standardized months when evaluated under a complete month-by-half-hour design. Conversely, DCFS remained sufficiently negative in the standardized predictions to be classified as a consistent sink.

### When, where, and how large CH4 fluxes are expected to be

The timing analysis showed that most sites had positive model-standardized annual budgets, but the seasonal pattern and source-month fraction varied substantially among sites. Monthly budget and source-probability heatmaps separated three useful interpretive dimensions: whether a site was consistently positive across months, whether it changed sign seasonally, and whether its annual magnitude was large or close to zero.

The consistency and magnitude analysis screened 74 usable site attributes, expanding the category comparison to include additional canopy structure variables. The strongest univariate association with annual budget was mean annual precipitation (Spearman rho = 0.440), followed by mean annual temperature (rho = 0.331). Thus, wetter and warmer sites tended to have larger positive annual CH4 budgets in the model-standardized analysis. Source-month fraction was more closely associated with soil texture and soil depth than with climate: it increased with sand content (rho = 0.290) and decreased with biogeochemical top depth (rho = -0.281), silt content (rho = -0.273), and biogeochemical bottom depth (rho = -0.256). Silt content was also the strongest univariate separator of consistent versus fluctuating groups in the screening tests (p = 0.0438).

Canopy variables did not dominate the category contrasts, but several canopy structure metrics were retained among the higher-ranked predictors for annual magnitude. Canopy vertical dispersion, canopy height, canopy height model standard deviation, mean canopy height model value, and upper canopy quantile metrics all showed positive but moderate associations with annual budget or source-month fraction. These canopy associations were weaker than the climate association with annual budget and weaker than the soil-texture association with source-month fraction, but they suggest that more vertically developed vegetation structure may covary with larger expected CH4 source strength at some sites.

The exploratory consistency tree had 82% leave-one-site-out accuracy for classifying sites as consistent or fluctuating. Most classification errors occurred between consistent and fluctuating groups near the threshold rather than among strongly positive or strongly negative sites. The regression tree for annual budget had a leave-one-site-out RMSE of 0.502 g C m-2 yr-1 and MAE of 0.409 g C m-2 yr-1. Tree variable-importance scores emphasized silt content for both consistency and magnitude. In the consistency tree, silt content was the dominant variable, followed by biogeochemical bottom depth, sand content, biogeochemical top depth, and mean annual precipitation. In the magnitude tree, silt and sand content had nearly equal importance, followed by mean annual temperature, biogeochemical bottom depth, mean annual precipitation, canopy vertical dispersion, biogeochemical top depth, canopy height, and canopy height model standard deviation.

Taken together, the driver summaries point to two related but distinct questions. The probability that a site is consistently a source rather than fluctuating was most closely described by soil texture and depth variables, especially silt, sand, and biogeochemical depth. The expected annual magnitude was more strongly related to climate, especially precipitation and temperature, while still retaining soil texture and canopy structure as secondary predictors. In practical terms, the current analysis suggests that source consistency is most strongly associated with where a site falls in soil-attribute space, whereas source magnitude is more strongly associated with climate gradients and then modified by soil and canopy structure.

### What likely makes a site a source, sink, or fluctuating through time

The clearest interpretation is that source versus sink behavior reflects both a site's baseline environmental setting and the seasonal conditions imposed on that setting. A site is more likely to behave as a sustained CH4 source when its underlying site attributes favor repeated positive monthly budgets and when seasonal conditions do not push the site back across zero. In this analysis, sustained source behavior was most consistently associated with soil texture/depth attributes and warmer conditions. The model-standardized results suggest that source consistency increased at sites with soil attributes associated with higher sand and lower silt/depth effects, while source magnitude increased most strongly along wetter and warmer climate gradients. This implies that the conditions that determine whether a site is positive in most months are not identical to the conditions that determine how large the annual source is once it is positive.

Mechanistically, consistent sources are likely sites where CH4 production or transport is favored often enough that monthly net flux remains positive through most of the year. Warmer temperatures can increase microbial activity, while wetter conditions can maintain more anaerobic microsites that support methanogenesis. Soil texture and depth likely matter because they influence water retention, gas diffusion, redox structure, and the vertical distribution of reactive soil material. Canopy structure may contribute indirectly by covarying with productivity, rooting, shading, evapotranspiration, and organic inputs, but canopy metrics were secondary predictors relative to climate and soil attributes.

Fluctuating sites are likely closer to the balance point between CH4 production, oxidation, and transport. At these sites, seasonal or diel conditions can move the net flux above or below zero. They may become sources during warmer or wetter periods, or when plant-mediated transport and production are high, but shift toward weak source or sink behavior when soils are drier, cooler, more oxygenated, or when oxidation offsets production. In the observed-category comparison, fluctuating sites tended to have higher dry mass, higher acidity/pH-variable values, higher C:N, higher estimated organic carbon, and deeper biogeochemical sampling depths than consistent-source sites. These attributes do not prove mechanism, but they suggest that fluctuating sites occupy soil settings where net flux is more sensitive to seasonal state or where production and oxidation are more closely balanced.

Sink behavior is inferred when the standardized monthly or annual net budget is negative, meaning CH4 uptake or oxidation exceeds CH4 production and release over the modeled period. In this run, no observed balanced monthly sites were classified as consistent sinks, and only one site, DCFS, was classified as a consistent sink under the model-standardized annual analysis. This means the data provide limited leverage for identifying sink conditions across sites. The likely conditions for sink behavior are cooler, drier, and more oxygenated soils, or soils where CH4 oxidation remains strong relative to production and transport. Because only one standardized sink was identified, the sink interpretation should be treated as a hypothesis rather than a fitted cross-site rule.

In practical terms, a site's expected behavior can be read in three steps. First, use the standardized monthly budgets to determine whether the site is usually positive, usually negative, or seasonally crossing zero. Second, use soil texture/depth attributes to understand whether the site is structurally likely to be consistent or near the switching threshold. Third, use climate, especially precipitation and temperature, to interpret the likely magnitude of the annual budget. Sites that are warm and wet but also structurally favorable for repeated positive monthly fluxes are expected to be the strongest and most consistent sources. Sites near zero monthly budgets, or with soil settings that make production and oxidation more evenly balanced, are expected to fluctuate through time.

### Monthly sink behavior

Because consistent sink sites were rare, we also evaluated sink behavior at the site-month scale. This showed that monthly sink states were much more common than site-level sink classes. In the observed balanced monthly data, 289 of 1,170 site-months were net sinks (24.7%). In the model-standardized monthly budgets, 52 of 468 site-months were net sinks (11.1%). Thus, monthly sink behavior occurs across many sites, but model standardization reduces the frequency of inferred sink months, suggesting that some observed sink months are sensitive to sampling coverage while a smaller set of seasonal sink months persists under a fully balanced design.

Monthly sink behavior was seasonally structured. In the observed balanced monthly summaries, sink months were most frequent in summer (34% of site-months), compared with winter (23%), autumn (22%), and spring (20%). The model-standardized budgets showed the same broad seasonal tendency but with lower overall frequency: summer had the highest standardized sink fraction (20%), followed by winter (13%), autumn (7%), and spring (5%). This means that sink behavior should not be interpreted only as a cold-season phenomenon. In these data, monthly sink states often occurred during warmer parts of the year.

The strongest immediate distinction between monthly sink and source states was the gradient component of the total flux. Observed sink months had a median monthly gradient component of -0.0197 mg C m-2 30 min-1, whereas source months had a median gradient component of 0.0249 mg C m-2 30 min-1. Storage was secondary: storage components were near zero for both sink and source months, and storage absolute fraction was only slightly higher in sink months. This indicates that monthly sink behavior was primarily driven by the gradient flux becoming negative, not by storage flux overwhelming a positive gradient.

Within-site seasonal anomalies also mattered. Observed sink months occurred in months that were warmer than the site's own average month: the median within-site temperature anomaly was +1.98 degrees C in sink months and -0.89 degrees C in source months. The same tendency appeared in model-standardized monthly budgets, where standardized sink months had a median within-site temperature anomaly of +2.20 degrees C. Mean VSWC and within-site VSWC anomalies were weak separators in both observed and standardized monthly summaries. This suggests that, at the monthly scale represented here, sink behavior is not simply explained by lower mean soil moisture. Instead, sink months may reflect periods when CH4 oxidation, gas diffusion, or reduced production/transport outweigh methanogenesis, even during relatively warm months.

The monthly analysis also identified a sampling issue that matters for interpretation. Observed sink months had fewer half-hour bins than source months (median 17 versus 33 bins), and half-hour bin count was a strong term in the monthly sink-state model. This does not invalidate the observed sink months, but it means that monthly sink inference should be interpreted alongside the model-standardized budgets. The standardized analysis supports the conclusion that sink months are real and seasonally concentrated, but it gives a more conservative estimate of their frequency.

The sites with the highest observed monthly sink frequency were mostly sites classified as fluctuating at the site level, including BLAN, UKFS, BONA, HEAL, DEJU, WREF, ONAQ, and SERC. This supports the interpretation that fluctuating sites are not random mixtures of noisy measurements; rather, many have repeated months where net CH4 uptake exceeds production and release. A site becomes fluctuating when these sink months are frequent enough to interrupt otherwise positive behavior but not frequent enough to make the site a consistent sink.

### ERA5-driven half-hourly annual budgets

The ERA5-driven half-hourly gapfill used 131,354 observed flux records with matched ERA5 covariates. The half-hourly GAM explained 2.0% of the deviance in total CH4 flux, similar to the original 30-minute total-flux model. Smooth terms for hour of day, day of year, ERA5 air temperature, ERA5 soil volumetric water content, the ERA5 temperature by soil-moisture interaction, and site identity were all significant. This indicates that the ERA5 covariates captured useful temporal and environmental structure, although most half-hourly flux variability remained unexplained.

The ERA5-driven annual budgets were strongly consistent with the monthly-bin annual budgets. Across 39 sites, the Spearman correlation between ERA5 half-hourly budgets and monthly-bin budgets was 0.935. The mean ERA5-minus-monthly-bin difference was -0.0507 g C m-2 yr-1, indicating that the ERA5 half-hour gapfill was slightly lower on average. The RMSE between the two budget estimates was 0.122 g C m-2 yr-1. Annual source/sink sign agreed for 38 of 39 sites.

The largest differences occurred at a small number of sites. RMNP had a lower ERA5-driven budget than monthly-bin budget (0.221 versus 0.673 g C m-2 yr-1; difference = -0.452), BONA changed sign between methods (ERA5 = -0.126; monthly-bin = 0.112; difference = -0.239), and LAJA had a higher ERA5-driven budget than monthly-bin budget (0.821 versus 0.607; difference = 0.214). Other notable negative differences occurred at JERC, NIWO, BLAN, UKFS, BART, and WREF, while TALL had a higher ERA5-driven budget than monthly-bin budget.

Overall, the ERA5 comparison supports the monthly-bin budget as a robust site-ranking and sign estimate for most sites, while showing that annual magnitude can shift when half-hourly meteorological and soil-moisture variability is used directly. The monthly-bin approach provides a balanced seasonal/diel expectation, whereas the ERA5 half-hour gapfill uses continuous year-specific covariate trajectories and retains observed fluxes where available. Agreement between the two methods suggests that the main conclusions about consistent sources, fluctuating sites, and annual magnitude are not an artifact of the monthly-bin extrapolation alone. The few sites with large differences are useful targets for sensitivity checks because they may be more dependent on the timing of observed fluxes, ERA5 covariate trajectories, or near-zero annual budgets.

Together, these results suggest that consistency is best understood from the standardized monthly sign pattern, whereas expected magnitude is best interpreted from annual model-standardized budgets with uncertainty. Site attributes provide useful descriptive structure, especially soil texture and climate, but the small number of sites and covariation among attributes mean the tree results should be used as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

## Supplement: Site-Attribute Comparisons for Total-Flux Behavior Categories

### Supplementary methods

The `NEON.StrongSink.DriverComparison.R` script was used to describe environmental and site-attribute differences among behavior categories. Because the current total-flux classification contained no observed consistent sink sites, the script compared the two classes present in the observed balanced monthly summaries: consistent sources versus fluctuating sites. This analysis was therefore not a driver model of 30-minute fluxes; it was a site-level comparison of attributes associated with the categories produced by `flow.30min.analysis.R`.

Site-level moisture summaries were calculated from the 30-minute model data and monthly site summaries. These included mean volumetric soil water content, mean soil-water-content variance, monthly soil-water-content standard deviation, and monthly soil-water-content range. These moisture summaries were joined with the site behavior table, which already contained climate, canopy, and soil attributes. Soil variables included numeric columns from the soil biogeochemistry/root biomass file, including sulfur, depth variables, carbon, nitrogen, C:N ratio, estimated organic carbon, acidity, bulk density, sand, silt, clay, and dry mass. Climate variables included mean annual precipitation and mean annual temperature. Vegetation and canopy variables included LAI, canopy height, and canopy height model summaries.

For each site attribute, values were compared between consistent-source and fluctuating sites using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Cliff's delta was calculated as a nonparametric effect size, where positive values indicated higher values in consistent-source sites and negative values indicated lower values in consistent-source sites. P values were adjusted across tested variables using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Because the number of sites was small and many attributes covary, these tests were interpreted as exploratory category descriptions rather than independent tests of causal mechanisms.

### Supplementary results

The strongest category contrasts were dominated by soil physical and soil chemical attributes, with one climate contrast. Consistent-source sites had lower dry mass than fluctuating sites (median 2.25 versus 3.41; Cliff's delta = -0.411; p = 0.029; BH-adjusted p = 0.318) and lower pH/acidity values (8.08 versus 17.4; Cliff's delta = -0.389; p = 0.039; BH-adjusted p = 0.318). Mean annual temperature was higher in consistent-source sites than fluctuating sites (12.9 versus 8.6 degrees C; Cliff's delta = 0.366; p = 0.053; BH-adjusted p = 0.318). Consistent-source sites also tended to have lower C:N ratio (8.98 versus 14.1; Cliff's delta = -0.358; p = 0.058; BH-adjusted p = 0.318), lower estimated organic carbon (14.3 versus 22.5; Cliff's delta = -0.300; p = 0.112), and shallower biogeochemical top depth (26.7 versus 30.3; Cliff's delta = -0.263; p = 0.164).

The broader set of contrasts showed that soil chemistry/depth variables had the most consistent directional pattern. Total C and sulfur were both lower in consistent-source sites than fluctuating sites (medians 0.169 versus 0.217; Cliff's delta = -0.258), and biogeochemical bottom depth was also lower in consistent-source sites (49.2 versus 52.4; Cliff's delta = -0.237). Total N was lower in consistent-source sites (1.55 versus 1.92; Cliff's delta = -0.221). Soil texture variables were more mixed: clay was higher in consistent-source sites (18.0 versus 15.7; Cliff's delta = 0.189), silt was slightly higher (33.2 versus 31.1; Cliff's delta = -0.058, indicating weak rank separation despite the median difference), and sand was lower (47.4 versus 52.4; Cliff's delta = -0.042). These texture contrasts were weaker in the observed-category comparison than in the model-standardized consistency/magnitude screening.

Moisture contrasts were small. Mean VSWC was nearly identical between groups (0.116 in consistent-source sites versus 0.112 in fluctuating sites; Cliff's delta = 0.005), and wetness seasonality had weak negative contrasts for consistent-source sites. Mean VSWC variance was lower in consistent-source sites (1.78e-4 versus 3.81e-4; Cliff's delta = -0.226), suggesting somewhat less variable soil moisture, but this contrast was weaker than the leading soil chemistry and temperature contrasts.

Vegetation and canopy variables provided secondary context rather than strong separation. LAI was lower in consistent-source sites (0.883 versus 1.43; Cliff's delta = -0.137), whereas canopy height was higher (11.5 versus 6.10 m; Cliff's delta = 0.129). CHM was also higher in consistent-source sites by median value, although rank separation was weak. These canopy patterns are consistent with the later consistency-magnitude screening, where canopy structure metrics contributed to annual magnitude but were not the strongest separators of behavior category.

No contrasts remained significant after Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment, so these results should be read as evidence of candidate conditions that describe the categories rather than as confirmed predictors. The clearest pattern was that observed consistent-source sites tended to occupy warmer sites with lower values for several soil mass, acidity, and organic-matter-related variables than fluctuating sites. Moisture and canopy differences were present but weaker and less internally consistent. These category descriptions complement the model-standardized analyses: the driver-comparison script describes how observed classes differ in site-attribute space, while the gapfill and consistency-magnitude scripts evaluate behavior and annual budget under a balanced month-by-hour design.
