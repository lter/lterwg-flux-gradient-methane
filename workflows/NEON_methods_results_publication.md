# Methods

## NEON CH4 Flux Processing

We analyzed methane (CH4) fluxes from NEON tower-gradient observations using a total-flux approach that combined turbulent gradient flux and storage. For each half-hour observation, total CH4 flux was calculated as the sum of the ensemble gradient flux (`FG_ENSEMBLE_RSHP`) and the filled storage flux (`storage_flux_filled`). Fluxes were converted to carbon units as mg C m-2 per 30 min using the conversion factor `2 * 0.0000288872 * 1000`. Positive fluxes were interpreted as net CH4 emission to the atmosphere, hereafter source states, and negative fluxes were interpreted as net CH4 uptake, hereafter sink states.

Because half-hour observations were unevenly distributed across sites, months, and times of day, site behavior was classified using a balanced temporal summary rather than raw observation counts. For each site-month, observations were first averaged within each of the 48 half-hour-of-day bins. Monthly mean flux was then calculated from the equally weighted half-hour bins. This ensured that site behavior was not dominated by more frequently sampled periods of the day. A site-month was classified as a source month when its balanced mean total flux was positive. Site-level behavior was then assigned from the fraction of source months: sites with at least 75% source months were classified as consistent sources, sites with no more than 25% source months were classified as consistent sinks, and all other sites were classified as fluctuating.

Generalized additive models (GAMs) were not used to balance the observed data. Balancing was performed directly by equal weighting of observed half-hour bins within site-months. GAMs were fit after this classification step to describe environmental and diel structure in half-hourly fluxes and to support subsequent budget extrapolation. The continuous half-hour model used total CH4 flux in mg C m-2 per 30 min as the response. A parallel binomial model used source state as the response. Predictor terms included hour-of-day structure, air temperature, soil volumetric water content, log-transformed photosynthetically active radiation, a temperature-by-moisture interaction, season, ecosystem type, and a site random effect.

## Site Attributes Associated With CH4 Behavior

We evaluated whether site characteristics differed between observed behavior classes. Because no site met the consistent-sink criterion under the observed total-flux classification, the comparison contrasted consistent source sites with fluctuating sites. Candidate drivers included climate, soil chemistry, soil physical properties, vegetation and canopy attributes, and site-level moisture summaries. Moisture summaries were calculated from the same site-month-hour balanced data structure used for behavior classification before reducing to site-level statistics.

For each candidate driver, we calculated class medians and nonparametric effect sizes using Cliff's delta. We also calculated nominal rank-based p-values and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-values. These contrasts were interpreted as exploratory because the number of sites was limited and many site attributes covary.

## Model-Standardized Annual Budgets

We estimated annual CH4 budgets from the half-hourly total-flux GAM using a standardized month-by-hour prediction grid. The grid included every site, all 12 calendar months, and all 48 half-hour-of-day bins. Environmental covariates were filled hierarchically using observed medians. Site-month-hour medians were used where available; missing combinations were filled from broader summaries in the following order: site-month, site-season-hour, site-season, site, global-month-hour, global-month, and global median values. This produced a common temporal template for comparing sites while retaining site- and season-specific covariate information when supported by the observations.

Predicted half-hour fluxes were summed across the 48 half-hour bins and multiplied by the number of days in each month to estimate monthly budgets. Annual budgets were calculated as the sum of monthly budgets and converted to g C m-2 yr-1. Model-estimation uncertainty was propagated with 1,000 simulations from the fitted GAM coefficient covariance matrix. These intervals describe uncertainty in the expected model-standardized budget conditional on the fitted model and covariate grid; they do not include all uncertainty associated with measurement error, covariate imputation, storage estimation, or model structure.

Model-standardized monthly behavior was defined from the fraction of predicted monthly budgets that were positive. Annual-budget behavior was defined from the simulated probability that the annual budget was positive. Source, sink, and fluctuating classes used the same 75% and 25% thresholds as the observed behavior classification.

## ERA5-Driven Half-Hourly Annual Budgets

As an independent annual-budget analysis, we estimated continuous half-hourly annual budgets using ERA5 meteorological forcing. Hourly ERA5 point covariates were obtained for each NEON tower coordinate through the Open-Meteo Archive API and cached for reproducibility. We extracted 2 m air temperature and 0-7 cm soil volumetric water content, then linearly interpolated hourly values to the half-hour grid for each site-year represented in the flux observations.

We fit a half-hourly GAM to observed total CH4 fluxes with matching ERA5 covariates. The model included cyclic smooths for hour of day and day of year, smooth terms for ERA5 air temperature and ERA5 soil moisture, a tensor interaction between temperature and moisture, season, ecosystem type, and a site random effect. The response was total CH4 flux in mg C m-2 per 30 min. Model diagnostics were summarized with observed-versus-fitted fluxes, residuals, and population-level partial effects for the ERA5 covariates.

ERA5 annual budgets retained observed half-hour fluxes where available and filled missing half-hours with ERA5-driven model predictions. For sites represented in the training data, prediction used the site random effect; otherwise, prediction used the population-level fixed-effect component. Half-hour fluxes were summed to annual site-year budgets and converted to g C m-2 yr-1. Site-level ERA5 annual behavior was classified from the fraction of site-years with positive annual budgets. Sites were classified as consistent sources when at least 75% of ERA5 annual budgets were positive, consistent sinks when no more than 25% were positive, and fluctuating otherwise. We compared ERA5 annual budgets with both the model-standardized 30-minute annual budget and the lookup-filled scaled annual budget. Final ERA5 figures summarized scaled and ERA5 annual flux distributions by ERA5 annual class, diel flux patterns, diel source probability, site counts by ERA5 class, ecosystem-type composition, and geographic distribution of ERA5 annual behavior.

## Cross-Scale Driver Models

We evaluated drivers of CH4 source probability and flux magnitude at three temporal scales: 30 min, daily, and annual. The analysis was designed to separate within-site temporal drivers from among-site climate, trait, and ecosystem differences. This distinction was essential because short-term temporal variation and persistent site differences can otherwise be confounded.

Three model-ready data sets were generated. The 30-minute data set used observed total fluxes converted to umol C m-2 s-1 for native instantaneous-rate interpretation. The primary daily data set used lookup-filled daily fluxes on a complete 48 half-hour grid for each observed site-date. Observed half-hours were retained, and missing half-hours were filled from site month-hour means, then site season-hour means, site warm/cool bi-season-hour means, and site annual-hour means, with global fallbacks only where needed. Completed half-hour values were summed to daily rates in mg C m-2 d-1. The annual data set used ERA5-gapfilled site-year annual budgets in g C m-2 yr-1, paired with annual ERA5 covariates and site-level attributes.

At each scale, we fit a binomial model for source probability and a Gaussian model for log10 absolute flux magnitude. For 30-minute and daily models, temperature, soil moisture, and light were centered within site before modeling. Thus, within-site terms represented temporal departures from each site's typical conditions, while among-site differences were represented by ecosystem type, site structure, and site-level annual covariates. The 30-minute models included within-site air temperature, soil moisture, and log(PAR), hour of day, day of year, season, ecosystem type, and a site random effect. Daily models included within-site daily mean air temperature, soil moisture, and log(PAR), day of year, season, ecosystem type, and a site random effect. Annual models used ERA5 annual anomalies within site, ERA5 site means, mean annual precipitation, soil acidity, soil carbon, soil sulfur, and ecosystem type.

To account for temporal autocorrelation and uneven site representation, uncertainty in driver effects and variable importance was estimated with a blocked site-level bootstrap. Bootstrap replicates resampled sites with replacement and refit models using the resulting site weights. This approach avoided treating autocorrelated 30-minute or daily observations as independent replicates. Variable importance was summarized from full-model term statistics across bootstrap replicates, and partial-effect curves were generated by varying each focal driver while holding other covariates at representative values and excluding site random effects where appropriate.

## Comparison With Published CH4 Flux Benchmarks

We compared NEON daily CH4 fluxes with published ecosystem-scale, soil-chamber, and process-model reference rates. NEON daily values were calculated from all observed site-days using the sampling-adjusted daily total described above, then summarized by site and behavior class. Published FLUXNET-CH4 ecosystem-class annual means from Delwiche et al. (2021) were converted from g C m-2 yr-1 to mg C m-2 d-1. Upland soil CH4 uptake rates from the MeMo v1.0 process model were converted from mg CH4 m-2 yr-1 to mg C m-2 d-1 and assigned negative signs to represent uptake.

We also compiled soil-chamber benchmarks for wetland emissions and upland uptake, including wetland, forest, desert, grassland, and cropland chamber studies. All chamber values were converted to mg C m-2 d-1 with positive values indicating net emission and negative values indicating net uptake. These comparisons placed NEON tower-gradient fluxes in the context of both ecosystem-scale wetland emissions and lower-magnitude soil-atmosphere exchange.

# Results

## Observed CH4 Behavior at the 30-Minute Scale

Balanced observed total-flux summaries classified 20 NEON sites as consistent CH4 sources and 19 sites as fluctuating. No site met the criterion for a consistent sink. A sensitivity analysis using gradient flux alone produced the same behavior classifications as the total-flux analysis, indicating that adding the filled storage term did not change site-level observed behavior under the current classification scheme.

The half-hourly GAMs explained modest but interpretable variation in CH4 dynamics. The continuous flux model explained 2.39% of deviance, whereas the binomial source-state model explained 5.50% of deviance. Thus, the probability of being a source or sink had clearer predictable structure than signed flux magnitude, but most half-hourly variability remained unexplained by the measured environmental and temporal covariates.

## Site Characteristics Associated With Source Behavior

Exploratory site-level comparisons suggested that consistent source sites differed from fluctuating sites in soil physical properties, soil chemistry, and climate. Consistent source sites had lower dry mass than fluctuating sites, with median values of 2.25 versus 3.41 and Cliff's delta of -0.411. Consistent source sites also had lower acidity-index values, with medians of 8.08 versus 17.4 and Cliff's delta of -0.389. Mean annual temperature tended to be higher at consistent source sites than at fluctuating sites, with medians of 12.9 versus 8.6 degrees C and Cliff's delta of 0.366. C:N ratio was also lower at source sites. These patterns were not interpreted as confirmatory after multiple-comparison adjustment, but they indicate that warmer sites with lower dry mass and distinct soil chemistry were more likely to behave as consistent CH4 sources.

## Annual Budgets From Model-Standardized Monthly Bins

Model-standardized annual extrapolation shifted many sites toward source behavior. Based on standardized monthly budgets, 33 sites were classified as consistent sources, five as fluctuating, and one as a consistent sink. Based on annual-budget source probability, 33 sites were classified as consistent sources, four as fluctuating, and two as consistent sinks. All 20 observed consistent source sites remained annual consistent sources. Among the 19 observed fluctuating sites, 13 shifted to annual consistent source behavior, four remained fluctuating, and two shifted to annual consistent sink behavior.

Annual budgets were generally small relative to published wetland ecosystem fluxes but positive for many sites. The largest model-standardized annual budgets were estimated for LENO (1.51 g C m-2 yr-1; 95% simulation interval 1.14 to 1.91), CLBJ (1.18; 0.755 to 1.61), GRSM (1.12; 0.966 to 1.27), NIWO (1.01; 0.474 to 1.54), OAES (0.971; 0.889 to 1.05), and NOGP (0.922; 0.845 to 1.00). These high-budget sites had annual source probabilities near or equal to one under the model-standardized simulation.

## ERA5 Annual-Budget Sensitivity Analysis

The ERA5-driven half-hourly gapfill used 131,360 observed half-hour records with matched ERA5 covariates. The model had an RMSE of 0.221 mg C m-2 per 30 min, a mean absolute error of 0.0909 mg C m-2 per 30 min, and an observed-fitted correlation of 0.142. Across 39 sites, mean ERA5 annual budgets ranged from -0.257 to 1.57 g C m-2 yr-1, with a median of 0.345 g C m-2 yr-1.

ERA5 annual budgets were strongly correlated with model-standardized 30-minute annual budgets (Spearman rho = 0.948). The mean ERA5-minus-model-standardized difference was -0.0505 g C m-2 yr-1, the RMSE was 0.117 g C m-2 yr-1, and budget sign agreed for 37 of 39 sites. Relative to the lookup-filled scaled annual classes, ERA5 annual classes agreed for 34 of 39 sites. Three scaled consistent sinks remained ERA5 consistent sinks, one scaled consistent sink became an ERA5 consistent source, one scaled fluctuating site remained fluctuating, one scaled fluctuating site became an ERA5 consistent sink, one scaled fluctuating site became an ERA5 consistent source, 30 scaled consistent sources remained ERA5 consistent sources, and two scaled consistent sources became ERA5 consistent sinks.

ERA5 annual behavior classified 32 sites as consistent sources, six as consistent sinks, and one as fluctuating. ERA5 consistent sinks included three forests, one grassland, one shrubland, and one wetland. The single ERA5 fluctuating site was a forest. ERA5 consistent sources included 18 forests, eight grasslands, four shrublands, and two croplands. ERA5 annual budgets were generally more positive for consistent sources and more negative for consistent sinks than the scaled annual budgets. Within ERA5 consistent source sites, the median scaled annual budget was 0.122 g C m-2 yr-1, whereas the median ERA5 annual budget was 0.422 g C m-2 yr-1. Within ERA5 consistent sink sites, the median scaled annual budget was -0.0304 g C m-2 yr-1, whereas the median ERA5 annual budget was -0.157 g C m-2 yr-1. These results indicate that ERA5 gap filling preserved broad site ranking and annual behavior for most sites, while increasing the magnitude of inferred annual source or sink strength for several sites.

## Cross-Scale Drivers of Source Probability and Flux Magnitude

The cross-scale driver analysis showed that controls on CH4 behavior depended on temporal scale. At the 30-minute scale, the source-probability model used 131,354 observations from 39 sites and explained 5.46% of deviance; the log absolute magnitude model explained 25.1% of deviance. At the daily scale, the source-probability model used 18,242 site-days and explained 6.77% of deviance; the magnitude model explained 21.6%. At the annual scale, the source-probability and magnitude models used 150 site-years and explained 48.0% and 22.8% of deviance, respectively.

Within-site temporal drivers dominated the 30-minute and daily models. For source probability, within-site PAR, soil moisture, air temperature, and day of year were supported at both 30-minute and daily scales, while hour of day was supported only at the 30-minute scale. For flux magnitude, within-site PAR, air temperature, soil moisture, and day of year were also supported at both subdaily and daily scales. Ecosystem type was supported across 30-minute, daily, and annual analyses for both source probability and flux magnitude, indicating a persistent among-site component that was not reducible to short-term temporal anomalies.

Annual-scale driver patterns differed from the 30-minute and daily patterns. Annual source probability was associated primarily with ecosystem type and among-site variables including acidity, mean annual precipitation, ERA5 site-mean soil moisture, ERA5 site-mean air temperature, and soil carbon. Annual flux magnitude was associated with ecosystem type, ERA5 site-mean soil moisture and air temperature, mean annual precipitation, acidity, and ERA5 annual anomalies. Because the annual analysis included only 150 site-years and used gapfilled annual budgets, these associations should be interpreted as predictors of net annual source outcome rather than direct short-term process controls.

## Comparison With Published Flux Benchmarks

NEON daily total CH4 fluxes were small relative to published FLUXNET-CH4 ecosystem-class fluxes. The NEON consistent source class had a median site-level daily flux of 0.626 mg C m-2 d-1, with an interquartile range of 0.355 to 0.943 mg C m-2 d-1. The NEON fluctuating class had a median daily flux of 0.0366 mg C m-2 d-1, with an interquartile range of 0.0136 to 0.117 mg C m-2 d-1. In contrast, published FLUXNET-CH4 ecosystem-class means ranged from 7.95 mg C m-2 d-1 for salt marshes to 127 mg C m-2 d-1 for urban sites.

Soil-chamber and process-model benchmarks indicated that NEON tower-gradient fluxes were closer in magnitude to upland soil-atmosphere exchange than to wetland ecosystem emissions. Wetland chamber emissions were represented by a benchmark of 75 mg C m-2 d-1, far above NEON class medians. Forest, desert, grassland, and cropland chamber benchmarks ranged from approximately -1.5 to -0.113 mg C m-2 d-1, overlapping the low-magnitude negative to weak-positive range of NEON daily site medians. MeMo process-model uptake estimates were also negative and generally ranged from approximately -1.24 to -0.216 mg C m-2 d-1 after conversion to daily C units.

## Synthesis

Across analyses, NEON CH4 behavior was characterized by frequent switching between weak source and sink states superimposed on small net annual budgets. Observed half-hourly data identified short-term source-state structure and diel/environmental effects, but half-hour observations were strongly autocorrelated and unevenly sampled. Daily source probability provided the most defensible scale for estimating realized source potential because it reduced half-hourly pseudoreplication while retaining temporal environmental variability. Annual monthly-bin and ERA5 analyses showed that many sites were net annual sources, but annual classifications collapsed short-term switching into net budget outcomes. Thus, daily source probability is the most appropriate scale for inference about source potential, whereas annual budgets are best interpreted as estimates of net source outcome.
