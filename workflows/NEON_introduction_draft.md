# Introduction

Methane (CH4) is the second most important anthropogenic greenhouse gas after carbon dioxide, with a global warming potential approximately 28–34 times that of CO2 over a 100-year horizon (Myhre et al. 2013). Global atmospheric CH4 concentrations have more than doubled since the pre-industrial era and continue to rise, yet significant uncertainty remains in the balance between sources and sinks that regulate the atmospheric budget (Saunois et al. 2020). Among the most consistent features of the global CH4 budget is the recognition that non-wetland upland soils represent a net sink for atmospheric CH4, removing an estimated 26–38 Tg CH4 yr⁻¹ from the atmosphere through microbial oxidation (Saunois et al. 2020; Murguia-Flores et al. 2018). This upland soil sink is among the few terrestrial CH4 fluxes that act in opposition to the dominant pattern of emissions and plays a meaningful role in moderating atmospheric concentrations. Our understanding of this sink, however, rests almost entirely on a foundation of soil-chamber measurements collected at the plot scale—a foundation that is increasingly showing cracks when compared with exchange measured at broader spatial and temporal scales.

The case for the non-wetland upland soil CH4 sink was built through decades of manual and automated soil-chamber studies conducted across forests, grasslands, shrublands, and croplands worldwide. These campaigns established that aerobic upland soils harboring high-affinity methanotrophic bacteria routinely oxidize atmospheric CH4 at rates ranging from tenths to several mg CH4 m⁻² d⁻¹ (Adamsen and King 1993; Mosier et al. 1991; Striegl et al. 1992; Groffman and Pouyat 2009). Chamber studies demonstrated that soil moisture, soil temperature, texture, and land-use history modulate uptake rates in consistent and predictable ways, providing the mechanistic basis for process-model representations of methanotrophy (Del Grosso et al. 2000; Murguia-Flores et al. 2018). Regional and global extrapolations from these chamber datasets, combined with process models constrained by them, have converged on the view that non-wetland upland soils are a globally significant atmospheric sink, comparable in magnitude to some national-scale anthropogenic emission sources (Saunois et al. 2020). The confidence in this estimate derives not from any single measurement campaign but from the concordance of independent chamber-based datasets collected across biomes and decades.

Despite this convergence, the chamber approach carries inherent limitations that are difficult to evaluate without independent measurement at larger scales. Soil chambers sample the gas exchange at the soil surface—soil-air interface flux—and by design exclude aboveground canopy-atmosphere exchange, lateral transport, and any non-soil biotic pathways. Chambers are also typically deployed under favorable logistical conditions, potentially undersampling episodic emission events associated with soil disturbance, freeze-thaw transitions, or precipitation pulses that may temporarily reverse net uptake (Priemé and Christensen 1997; Goldberg et al. 2010). Spatial heterogeneity in soil moisture, root density, and methanotroph community structure can cause plot-scale chamber means to diverge substantially from landscape-scale averages, and upscaling from a small number of chamber collars to ecosystem and regional budgets introduces compounding uncertainty (Verchot et al. 2000). These limitations do not invalidate chamber-based estimates but they do suggest that the global upland CH4 sink could be systematically biased if the conditions most conducive to measuring uptake are also those most likely to be sampled.

Eddy covariance and gradient-based micrometeorological methods offer a complementary perspective on CH4 exchange by integrating flux signals over large footprints (typically 10⁴–10⁵ m²) that encompass canopy, soil, and understory pathways simultaneously. The rapid expansion of continuous ecosystem-scale CH4 flux measurements, documented by the FLUXNET-CH4 synthesis (Delwiche et al. 2021), has confirmed the dominant role of wetlands as CH4 sources at the ecosystem scale and revealed a diversity of flux behavior in upland systems that was not fully anticipated from chamber campaigns alone. Studies at individual upland sites have reported weak net CH4 emissions at the ecosystem scale under conditions where co-located chambers detected net uptake, suggesting that non-soil pathways—including plant-mediated transport, aerenchyma, and episodic lateral inputs—may contribute to ecosystem-scale exchange in ways that chamber measurements miss (Zona et al. 2009; Baldocchi et al. 2012; Wik et al. 2016). More broadly, ecosystem-scale measurements at upland sites show greater variability in the direction and magnitude of net CH4 exchange than the soil-sink paradigm would predict, with some temperate and tropical upland forests intermittently functioning as net CH4 sources at annual timescales (Gut et al. 2002; Varner et al. 2017).

The growing discrepancy between chamber-based expectations and ecosystem-scale observations raises a fundamental question: is the non-wetland upland soil sink, as currently represented in global budgets, an accurate characterization of net ecosystem CH4 exchange, or does it reflect the perspective of a measurement approach that is optimized to detect a process—soil methanotrophy—while remaining blind to others? Answering this question requires systematic characterization of ecosystem-scale CH4 exchange across a representative sample of upland ecosystems, sustained long enough to capture interannual variability and annual budgets, and analyzed in a framework that explicitly acknowledges the differences in what chambers and towers measure. Here we present such an analysis using tower-gradient CH4 flux measurements from the National Ecological Observatory Network (NEON), which operates standardized gradient-flux installations at 39 upland and one wetland site spanning a broad range of North American ecosystem types, climates, and land uses. We use these data to characterize site-level exchange behavior, estimate annual CH4 budgets, and upscale NEON-trained relationships to global non-wetland upland land area using ERA5 reanalysis meteorology. Our results provide the first multi-site, multi-year characterization of ecosystem-scale upland CH4 exchange at a continental scale, and they suggest that ecosystem-scale measurements resolve a more complex picture of upland CH4 exchange than the soil-sink paradigm implies.
