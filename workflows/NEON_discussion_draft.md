# Discussion

## What if the terrestrial methane sink is not as large as we think?

The results presented here raise a question that extends well beyond the scope of any single measurement network: what are the implications if the non-wetland upland terrestrial CH4 sink is substantially smaller than current estimates, or if upland terrestrial systems are, in net, a source rather than a sink? The answer is not simply a matter of revising a single budget term. It would require a fundamental reassessment of how global methane budgets are constructed, how process-based models are parameterized, and how we project the methane-climate feedback over the coming century.

### Budget rebalancing and the constraint of atmospheric observations

The global methane budget is anchored by directly observed atmospheric CH4 concentrations, which means that any revision to one budget component must be compensated elsewhere. Current bottom-up and top-down syntheses estimate that non-wetland upland soils remove approximately 20–40 Tg CH4 yr⁻¹ from the atmosphere, making the terrestrial soil sink one of the largest individual terms on the sink side of the global budget (Saunois et al. 2020). If the true terrestrial sink were substantially smaller, or if upland ecosystems were in aggregate a net source, then one of three adjustments would be necessary: anthropogenic or natural CH4 sources are currently overestimated, another unrecognized or underestimated sink exists, or atmospheric CH4 growth is being sustained by a larger net imbalance than current budgets acknowledge. Because the atmosphere integrates across all surface fluxes, the global budget must still close—reducing the terrestrial soil sink by 20–40 Tg yr⁻¹ would propagate as an equivalent discrepancy that must be resolved in other budget components. The location and nature of that resolution would reshape our understanding of which sectors and processes dominate the current imbalance between CH4 sources and sinks.

### Scaling from NEON towers and the structural bias of chamber-calibrated models

The upscaling analysis presented here suggests that ecosystem-scale net CH4 exchange in non-wetland upland systems is not consistently negative and that the spatial distribution of source and sink states depends strongly on which assumptions are embedded in the flux-expression step. These results do not require upland ecosystems to be major CH4 sources in absolute terms. Even near-zero ecosystem-scale fluxes, if they systematically offset the soil oxidation signal that chambers detect, would be sufficient to substantially erode the apparent terrestrial sink. A globally distributed network of ecosystem-scale flux measurements would be needed to determine whether this pattern is robust across biomes and climate zones (Watts et al. 2026). In the interim, the disparity between plot-scale chamber observations and the ecosystem-scale perspective offered by tower-gradient measurements points toward a structural problem rather than a parameter problem in current models.

Process-based models built and calibrated on decades of soil-chamber flux data are likely to carry the limitations of that measurement basis into their global projections. If chambers preferentially observe the oxidation-dominated portions of ecosystems—where surface soils are aerobic, accessible, and free from confounding soil moisture or disturbance—then models trained on those observations may systematically overestimate methanotrophy at the landscape scale. At the same time, cryptic methanogenesis occurring in anaerobic microsites, plant-mediated transport from deeper soil layers, and lateral CH4 redistribution through groundwater and hydrological connectivity are not captured by closed-chamber deployments and are correspondingly underrepresented in model structures calibrated to them. The result is a class of models that are internally consistent with their training data but may be structurally biased with respect to the full suite of ecosystem pathways that determine net CH4 exchange at the scales relevant to regional and global budgets. Correcting this would require not just updated parameter values but revised model architectures that can represent the full vertical and lateral extent of CH4 production, transport, and oxidation within an ecosystem.

### Methane-climate feedbacks and the buffering capacity of upland soils

The terrestrial soil sink has an additional significance beyond its magnitude: it is commonly treated as a negative feedback on atmospheric CH4 accumulation, because higher atmospheric concentrations are expected to stimulate greater microbial oxidation and therefore larger uptake rates (Ridgwell et al. 1999). If the terrestrial sink is weaker than assumed, this buffering capacity is proportionally smaller. The consequences would compound over time. A reduced sink would imply that the effective atmospheric lifetime of CH4 is longer than typically estimated, since a smaller fraction of emitted CH4 is removed per unit time by terrestrial oxidation. In turn, future increases in CH4 emissions—from warming-induced wetland expansion, permafrost thaw, or agricultural intensification—would produce larger atmospheric accumulation than models calibrated to the current sink magnitude would project. Climate projections that rely on these models may therefore underestimate CH4 accumulation under warming scenarios, potentially leading to an underestimate of the radiative forcing contribution from CH4 and an overestimate of the terrestrial biosphere's capacity to offset anthropogenic emissions through uptake.

Taken together, these considerations argue for treating the upland terrestrial CH4 sink as an open empirical question rather than a settled budget component. Resolving it will require sustained, ecosystem-scale CH4 flux measurements distributed across a range of biomes and climate states, methods that can attribute ecosystem-scale exchange to specific mechanistic pathways, and budget frameworks capable of formally integrating the different spatial and temporal scales at which chambers, towers, and atmospheric inversions operate. The NEON gradient-flux network represents one step in this direction, but the limitations of a North American–centric sample and a relatively short measurement record mean that the global picture remains incomplete. The most important contribution of this analysis may be to demonstrate that the assumption of a universal non-wetland upland CH4 sink—robust as it appears from decades of chamber data—warrants sustained scrutiny at the ecosystem scale before it is embedded as a fixed term in the next generation of global methane budget assessments and climate projections.
